## 0. TL;DR — the 12 rules that matter most

1. **The first frame is text on black, on screen by 0:00.3 (frame ~10 at 30 fps).** It is one sentence of 4–6 words in **past tense**, describes a **repeating routine**, and **implies that something will change**. No logo, no title card, no music swell before it.
2. **By 0:03 the viewer must recognize their own life**: a micro-ritual everyone does (alarm, snooze, scroll, cold bed, unread chat). The ritual is the hook made visible.
3. **By 0:08 the ritual must fail**, and a red one-word or two-word caption names the failure ("again."). That is the "this is me" moment. Self-recognition is the strongest retention glue in this format.
4. **By 0:13 the antagonist (red-glow shadow) and the DAY counter are both on screen.** The viewer now has a villain, a clock and a question: "how long will this go on?"
5. **The DAY counter is the progress bar.** It sits top-left in gray in every scene, jumps in big increments while he is failing (1 → 25 → 47 → 88 → 365) and then **resets to DAY 1**. That reset is the midpoint turn.
6. **The low point lands at about 35–38% of the runtime (0:50–0:58 of 2:39)**: spotlight, stillness, one white line of narration. It is the only long take in the film. Stillness after fast cutting works as a pattern interrupt.
7. **The turn is an exact visual callback to the opening shot** (same bedroom, same 5:00 clock) with **one changed variable**: a calm press instead of a slap, plus a red headband. The callback pays off the open loop the hook created.
8. **Act 3 is percussive.** Cuts land on the beat, every scene is a fresh environment, and every 8–15 s there is a "fight-back" beat (the inner-voice words return, and he defeats them).
9. **Each insult from act 1 comes back as a challenge in act 3** ("told you.", "you're lazy", "you can't"). Every insult is set up once and paid off once.
10. **The final boss is the shadow at its largest (~2:00)**, then it shrinks to a powerless figure in the bookend bedroom. The shadow's size works as an emotional gauge.
11. **The ending gives two shareable lines**: a narrator aphorism with an antithesis ("Motivation gets you started / Discipline keeps you going") and a closing chiasmus slogan ("Day one, or one day."). These are the lines people quote and screenshot.
12. **No voiceover, all meaning in on-screen text** (white = narrator, red = inner voice/failure, gray = day). The film works muted, works across languages, and every line can stand alone as a quote.

### 1.1 Why "Every day started the same." works (word by word)

| Element | Effect |
|---|---|
| **"Every day"** | Universal and repetitive. It describes the viewer's life, not a stranger's. It is also the second beat of the **Pixar/Kenn Adams Story Spine** ("Once upon a time… *Every day*… *But one day*…"). Audiences have been primed for centuries to expect "but one day" after "every day". The sentence promises a story arc without saying so. |
| **"started"** | Past tense. This says it is a story, not a lecture. It also implies the narrator knows how it ended, so it already happened and changed. Past tense creates a "what changed?" gap. |
| **"the same."** | Monotony as conflict. "The same" is quietly negative: nobody brags that every day is the same. It names a pain without a label like "procrastination" that would trigger defensiveness. |
| The full stop | Calm, final, a little ominous. No exclamation mark and no question mark. The film trusts the viewer. |
| 5 words, about 1.5 s to read | Readable before the thumb can swipe. It fits the YouTube "intro" window and works for muted autoplay on Home and Shorts-style feeds. |

**Psychological mechanisms at play**

| Mechanism | How the hook triggers it |
|---|---|
| **Self-recognition / self-reference** | Self-relevant information is processed and remembered better. The alarm and snooze ritual is near-universal, so the viewer stops being an audience member and becomes the subject. Identification with a protagonist increases "transportation" into the story, and in studies it even shifts self-beliefs toward the character (Sestir & Green; Krause & Appel). That is exactly the goal of a self-improvement film. |
| **Curiosity / information gap (Loewenstein 1994)** | "Started the same" leads to "until when? what broke the pattern?" The gap is small and feels resolvable, which is the size that keeps people watching. |
| **Implied transformation** | Past tense plus "every day" signals a before/after structure. The viewer already expects a "Day 1", and the title on the thumbnail (DISCIPLINE) confirms the promise. |
| **Time pressure** | A clock is the first prop (5:00 → 8:47). Clocks, day counters and calendars create a ticking-clock tension that runs through the whole film. |
| **Shame-lite humor** | "just 5 more minutes..." / "again." is gently funny. Light humor lowers defenses before the film turns heavy (the mirror, rock bottom). |
| **Title–thumbnail match** | YouTube defines "Intro" retention as the share of viewers still watching at 0:30 and ties a strong intro to the first 30 seconds matching the title and thumbnail promise. DISCIPLINE → alarm and snooze is a perfect, immediate match. |

### 1.2 The three-step hook pattern (reusable)
```
[1] STATEMENT on black  →  [2] RITUAL that viewers recognise  →  [3] RITUAL FAILS + one-word red verdict
     (story promise)          (self-recognition)                    (identity hit: "this is me")
     0:00–0:03                0:03–0:08                            0:08–0:13
```
Then within 5–8 seconds: **[4] antagonist + DAY counter** (the question "will he get out?" becomes the spine of the film).

## 2. Two hook archetypes: DISCIPLINE and DOPAMINE

| | **A. "Loop of Failure" (DISCIPLINE)** | **B. "Paradise Lost" (DOPAMINE)** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Text on black, then a recognizable failure ritual | Happy, colorful life with no text hook (painting, guitar, running, friend, kite) |
| Emotional start | Mild shame and humor | Warmth and joy |
| Hook question | "Will he ever break the loop?" | "What is going to take this away?" (dread) |
| Antagonist entry | 0:13 (the 10% mark) | about 0:15–0:40, the shadow handing over a phone with "just one more" |
| Low point | Spotlight, curled up (0:50) | Color drains to gray, lying in bed (≈1:20) |
| Turn | 5:00 alarm callback (1:00) | Day 1, about 1:23 (later, so the turn feels slower) |
| Color logic | Dark from the start; warm dawn as the reward | Color first, gray as punishment, color earned back as the reward |
| Closing slogan | "Day one, or one day." | "Cheap pleasure steals your color. Earn it back." |
| Result | **3.1M views** | **283K views** |

**Why A beat B by about 11x (hypotheses, ranked):**
1. **The first frame is a sentence, not a scene.** A is understood in 1.5 s, muted, at thumbnail size. B's first 10 s is pleasant but **asks no question**: a happy character painting is low stakes and gives no reason to stay.
2. **Self-recognition arrives at 0:03 in A; in B it arrives at about 0:15–0:20** (when the phone appears). Most "intro" drop-off happens in that gap.
3. **Negative-then-up arcs beat positive-then-down-then-up arcs over a short runtime.** B spends about 50% of the runtime (0:40–1:23) declining. A reaches the low point by 0:50 and spends 60% of the runtime rising, which feels like momentum and ends with high-arousal energy (Berger & Milkman: high-arousal emotions such as awe, anger and inspiration get shared, while low-arousal sadness does not).
4. **A's slogan is a chiasmus that implicates the viewer ("You decide").** B's slogan is descriptive.
5. **Topic breadth.** Everyone snoozes an alarm. Not everyone self-identifies as dopamine-addicted.

**Rule:** default to **Archetype A**. Use **B** only when the topic is defined by *loss* (grief, love/heartbreak, phone addiction, burnout). Even then, **graft A's text hook onto B**: open with a line on black ("He used to paint every day.") *and then* show the colorful life, so the past tense pre-loads the loss and turns the happy opening into dread. Hybrid recommendation for loss topics: `Text on black (past tense, implies loss) → 6–8 s of color/joy → first gray/loss sting by 0:12`.

Interrupt types used, so the skill can rotate them:
1. **Location change** (bedroom, couch, desk, mirror, street, floor, gym, stairs, summit)
2. **Scale change** (tiny hero vs. giant shadow; close-up clock)
3. **Tempo change** (fast montage, then stillness, then a drum hit)
4. **Palette change** (gray dark, blue phone, warm lamp, red bag glow, orange dawn)
5. **Typography event** (big red words, giant glowing counter, shattered letters)
6. **Sound event** (alarm, clack, glass crack, heartbeat, wind)
7. **Number change** (the DAY counter and the rep counter)

### 3.5 The DAY counter as a retention device
- **Position:** always top-left, gray, small caps, handwritten. Consistent, so the eye learns to check it like a progress bar.
- **Failure phase:** big, irregular jumps (1 → 25 → 47 → 88 → … → 365). This tells the viewer "time is being wasted," and it is a countdown to rock bottom.
- **Reset to DAY 1 at 1:00** is the midpoint pivot. The same text that opened the failure now opens the comeback. It is the most "rewatchable" device in the film.
- **Growth phase:** small early increments (3, 12) show that each day counts, then bigger jumps (64, 150, 190, 270) show momentum.
- The viewer subconsciously projects: "if DAY 270 is near the end, the film is close to the payoff." That is a progress bar that reduces quit risk in the final third.
- **Secondary counters** (push-up "1… 3… 5", big glowing number) use the same logic at micro scale and give a live "will he make it?" beat.

### 3.7 The rewatch / share moments (where spikes come from)
YouTube's "key moments" report labels **spikes** as segments people rewatch or share. Predicted spikes in this design:
1. **0:09 "again."**: the most screenshot-able, relatable frame (meme potential).
2. **0:55 "Lazy isn't who you are. It's what you repeat."**: a quotable reframe at the emotional low.
3. **1:30–1:35 words shatter on punch**: visual satisfaction (people scrub back to watch it again).
4. **1:49–2:04 walking through the giant shadow**: the climax.
5. **2:04 "Nobody is coming to save you. / So save yourself."**: the screenshot quote.
6. **2:25 "Day one, or one day."**: the share-caption line (it is also in the description, so people can copy it).

### 3.8 The closing slogan as a shareable object
- **Chiasmus / antimetabole** ("Day one, or one day") is short, symmetrical, easy to remember, and puts **the choice on the viewer**.
- Structure: `[TOPIC WORD, big, cream, red wavy underline]` + `[≤6-word chiasmus/antithesis, white, small]`.
- It must work **without the film** as a caption, comment or tattoo.
- Peak–end rule (Kahneman): people judge an experience mostly by its **peak** and its **end**. The film puts its biggest peak (shadow destroyed, summit) about 30 s before the end and finishes on a calm, high note plus a quote. The memory of the film becomes "that line".
- Slogan formulas: `X, or Y.` (chiasmus) · `A gets you started. B keeps you going.` (antithesis pair) · `Nobody is coming to X. So X yourself.` (negation, then imperative) · `X isn't who you are. It's what you repeat.` (identity reframe).

## 4. HOOK FORMULA LIBRARY

### 4.1 Hook-line templates (abstract)
| ID | Template | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | **"Every [time unit] [started/ended] the same."** | Story Spine "every day"; loop plus implied break |
| H2 | **"He [did ritual] every [time]. Just in case."** | Hope behind a habit, which is poignant |
| H3 | **"[Number] [unit]. [Same/zero] [result]."** | A number creates a progress-bar effect from frame one |
| H4 | **"He always [said/thought] '[excuse]'."** | The viewer's own excuse in quotes, which is confessional |
| H5 | **"Nobody noticed [subtle sign]."** | Implies hidden pain and a reveal |
| H6 | **"It was [time]. Again."** | Time pressure plus repetition in about 4 words |
| H7 | **"[Past tense statement]. Until [cut]."** | An explicit open loop |
| H8 | **"He used to [joyful thing]."** | Past tense implies loss (bridge to Archetype B) |
| H9 | **"[Object] was always [state]."** | Object-first image hook; strong in muted viewing |
| H10 | **"He was fine. Everybody said so."** | Dramatic irony |

Constraints for every hook line: **4–8 words**, **past tense** (or the present "again"), **no topic jargon** (never "Loneliness is…"), **no question marks** (a statement is more confident and story-like), **one full stop**, **readable in ≤2 s**.

**How to pair a hook with a ritual (rules):**
- The ritual must be **≤3 actions** and readable **without words** in a silhouette (reach, press, look, put down).
- It must involve **a prop that can come back** at the bookend (alarm clock, two mugs, phone, door, notebook, shoes, plate). **The prop is the bookend device.**
- The red verdict is **1 word plus a full stop** (or 2–3 words max), lower-case, red, set above the character.
- The "again." beat needs a **visible measure of failure**: a time jump on a clock, a cold mug, a dust layer, a stack growing.
- The shadow's first line must be **an excuse the viewer has personally said to themselves**: second-person, lower-case, short, and plausibly kind ("you're tired."), not cartoon-evil.

### 5.2 Shot length and cut-frequency budget per act

| Act | Time window | Max shot length | Target avg shot | Cuts per 10 s | Text beats | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 0:00–0:13 | 4 s | 3 s | 3 | 3 (hook, excuse, verdict) | The black card holds ≤3 s |
| **Act 1: the loop** | 0:13–0:40 | 8 s (the shadow intro only) | 2–3 s in the montage | 3–5 in the montage | One per vignette, red | DAY counter changes on **every** cut in the montage |
| **Act 1b: identity attack** | 0:40–0:50 | 6 s | 4–5 s | 2 | 1 red, big | Slow push-in, crack SFX |
| **Midpoint: stillness** | 0:50–1:00 | **10 s (the only long take)** | 8–10 s | 1 | 2 white lines | Particles plus a subtle camera drift keep it alive. Total stillness is OK for ≤10 s. |
| **Turn** | 1:00–1:12 | 4 s | 2 s | 4–5 | DAY 1 | Cut on the drum hit; close-up into wide |
| **Act 2: the grind** | 1:12–1:50 | 6 s | 3–4 s | 3 | One callback insult per vignette + 1 counter | Every vignette = new location/palette, cut on the beat |
| **Climax** | 1:50–2:05 | 7 s | 4–5 s | 2 | 1 aphorism pair + 1 red boss line | Bigger, fewer shots; scale is the drama |
| **Resolution** | 2:05–2:25 | 8 s | 6 s | 1–2 | 1 aphorism pair | Warm palette, wind, slow |
| **End card** | 2:25–end | ≥5 s hold | — | 0 | Title + slogan | No new information |

**Global rules:**
- **Nothing on screen is fully static for more than 2 s**: a blink, a breath, particles, glow pulsing or parallax drift is always moving.
- **A text beat roughly every 4–6 s** in acts 1 and 2 (narrator, inner voice, DAY or counter). In a voiceless film the text *is* the voice.
- **Text on screen for (word count × 0.3 s) + 0.8 s**, minimum 1.2 s, and it appears **after** the visual it comments on (the picture first, then the caption punchline).
- **One pattern interrupt every 8–15 s** (see §3.4 types). Never two of the same type in a row.
- **Act ratios:** Hook ≈ 8% · Loop/fall ≈ 25% · Bottom ≈ 6% · Turn ≈ 7% · Grind ≈ 25% · Climax ≈ 10% · Resolution + end ≈ 19%. The turn must land at **36–40%** of the runtime. Later than about 45% feels like a slog, which is the DOPAMINE problem.

## 6. RETENTION KILLERS (checklist: avoid all of these)

**First 15 seconds**
- [ ] Logo, channel intro, title card or "subscribe" ask before the hook.
- [ ] A hook line longer than 8 words, or one that needs a second read.
- [ ] A hook that **names the topic abstractly** ("Loneliness is an epidemic…"). That is a lecture, not a story.
- [ ] A question hook ("Do you ever feel lazy?"). It reads like an ad or self-help, and the viewer answers "no" and leaves.
- [ ] Opening on a happy, low-stakes scene without a text hook (the DOPAMINE problem).
- [ ] A ritual the audience doesn't share (too niche, culturally specific or adult-only).
- [ ] An opening that doesn't match the title/thumbnail promise within 3 s.
- [ ] Black screen held longer than 3 s, or music swell before the first word.

**Mid-film**
- [ ] The low point arriving after about 45% of the runtime (the fall drags).
- [ ] More than 10 s with no new information (no text, no cut, no counter change, no sound event).
- [ ] Two montages in a row with the same rhythm. Montage, then stillness, then a punch is required.
- [ ] Insults or setups that are never paid off, or payoffs with no setup.
- [ ] The antagonist being cartoon-evil. The shadow must sound like the viewer's own reasonable excuse.
- [ ] The DAY counter missing in some scenes or moving to different positions. Consistency is what makes it a progress bar.
- [ ] The turn happening without a cause. It needs the narrator reframe at rock bottom.
- [ ] The turn being an instant cure. He has to struggle (out of breath, almost failing at the push-up) or it feels fake.
- [ ] Preaching in act 1. Narrator lines (white) belong at the low point and in act 3, not during the fall.
- [ ] Text that is hard to read (thin font, low contrast, under 1.2 s, too many words, or placed over busy areas).
- [ ] Color used everywhere. If red means the inner voice, red must not be decoration (except earned objects like the punching bag), and warm orange is held back until the summit.

**Ending**
- [ ] Ending on the summit without the bookend. The loop opened at 0:00 has to be visibly closed.
- [ ] A slogan longer than 6–8 words, or two competing slogans.
- [ ] A long outro or end screen that starts before the slogan finishes.
- [ ] Ending sad or ambiguous in a motivational film. Low-arousal sadness spreads poorly (Berger & Milkman).
- [ ] Runtime past about 2:45 for this structure (the four-minute catalogue entries pull far fewer views; correlation, not proof).

**Packaging**
- [ ] Title not in the `TOPIC | An Animated Short Film` form (the formula behind the top two hits).
- [ ] Description that doesn't repeat the hook line in its first sentence and the aphorism. The DISCIPLINE description opens with the hook line, which makes it work as a preview text.
- [ ] Missing chapters. Chapters named after story beats ("The mirror", "Rock bottom", "Day 1") work as open loops in the description too.

## 7. Design rules for the skill (summary)

1. Generate the **hook line** from §4.1 templates under the constraints (4–8 words, past tense, no question, no topic word).
2. Choose a **prop-based micro-ritual** that can be bookended (§4.2 rules).
3. Lock in the **milestone table** (§5.1) as hard frame targets: hook ≤ frame 10, ritual by 90, excuse by 240, verdict + DAY 1 + shadow by 390, rock bottom at 36–38%, turn at 38–40%, final boss at ~70%, warm palette at ~78%, bookend at ~86%, end card at ~91%.
4. Write **every inner-voice line twice**: once as an act-1 excuse, once as an act-3 obstacle that gets destroyed (§3.6).
5. The DAY counter is mandatory: big jumps while failing, reset to DAY 1 at the turn, small then big jumps while growing.
6. The shadow's scale curve: medium → larger → reflection → **giant** → **tiny**.
7. Two narrator aphorism pairs (low point and summit) plus one chiasmus slogan, each ≤8 words per line.
8. Cut-frequency budget per act (§5.2) and a pattern interrupt every 8–15 s, rotating the seven types.
9. Validate against the retention-killer checklist (§6) before rendering.
